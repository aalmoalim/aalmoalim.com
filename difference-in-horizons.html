Difference in Horizons

The difference in the horizons of the new moon (ikhtilaf al-matali‘) is something that occurs between far-apart lands, just as the horizons of sunrise differ. But should this be taken into consideration when determining the beginning of Muslims’ fasting, and the timing of Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, and the other months—so that they differ among Muslims in beginning and end—or should it not be considered, so that Muslims are unified in their fasting and Eid?

This issue is: “If the people of one land sight the new moon, while others do not.” The jurists differed on it into two views:

First View: Differences in Horizons Are Not Considered

The Hanafis (in the relied-upon view), the Malikis (in the relied-upon view), some Shafi‘is, and the Hanbalis (in the relied-upon view) held that there is no weight given to differences in horizons. So if the people of one land sight the new moon, then fasting becomes binding upon all lands. They cite the statement of Allah, the Exalted: “So whoever witnesses the month among you, let him fast it” [Al-Baqarah 2:185], and the statement of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him:

“Fast when you see it, and break your fast when you see it.”(1)

They said: this hadith makes fasting obligatory by absolute sighting for all Muslims, without restricting it to a particular place.

Ibn Qudamah, may Allah have mercy on him, said: This day is from the month of Ramadan by the testimony of trustworthy people, therefore fasting it becomes obligatory upon all Muslims. And Ramadan is what lies between the two crescents; it has been established that this day is from it in other rulings—such as debts becoming due, divorce taking effect, emancipation, the obligation of vows, and other rulings—so fasting it is obligatory by text and consensus. And because an upright legal testimony has testified to the sighting of the new moon, fasting becomes obligatory, just as it would if the lands were close to each other.(2)

Second View: Differences in Horizons Are Considered When Lands Are Far Apart

The Shafi‘is (in the more correct view according to them), some Hanafis, some Malikis, and a view within the Hanbali school chosen by Shaykh al-Islam Ibn Taymiyyah, may Allah have mercy on him, held that if horizons differ and lands are far apart, fasting is not obligatory upon the people of the other land. But if lands are close, then their ruling is the ruling of one land, and the people of the other land must fast without disagreement.

They cite that Ibn ‘Abbas, may Allah be pleased with them both, did not act upon the sighting of the people of al-Sham, due to the hadith of Kurayb: Umm al-Fadl bint al-Harith sent him to Mu‘awiyah in al-Sham. He said: I arrived in al-Sham and fulfilled her need, and Ramadan began while I was in al-Sham. I saw the new moon on Friday night. Then I arrived in al-Madinah at the end of the month. ‘Abdullah ibn ‘Abbas, may Allah be pleased with them both, asked me, then mentioned the new moon and said: “When did you see the new moon?” I said: “We saw it on Friday night.” He said: “Did you see it yourself?” I said: “Yes, and the people saw it and fasted, and Mu‘awiyah fasted.” He said: “But we saw it on Saturday night, so we will continue fasting until we complete thirty days or we see it.” I said: “Will you not suffice with Mu‘awiyah’s sighting and his fasting?” He said: “No; this is how the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, commanded us.”(3)

Imam al-Nawawi’s Detailed Discussion

Imam al-Nawawi, may Allah have mercy on him, said: If they sight the new moon of Ramadan in one land and do not sight it in another, then if the two lands are close, their ruling is the ruling of one land, and the people of the other land must fast without disagreement. If they are far apart, then there are two well-known views in the two methodological approaches:

  • The more correct view: fasting is not obligatory upon the people of the other land. This was the decisive view of the author, Shaykh Abu Hamid, al-Bandaniji, and others; and it was deemed correct by al-‘Abdari, al-Rafi‘i, and most scholars.
  • The second view: it is obligatory. This was held by al-Saymari and was deemed correct by al-Qadi Abu al-Tayyib, al-Darimi, Abu ‘Ali al-Sinji, and others.

Those who held the second view responded to the hadith of Kurayb from Ibn ‘Abbas by saying: the sighting of the new moon in another land was not established to him by the testimony of two upright witnesses. And the correct view is the first.

Regarding what counts as “farness” and “nearness,” there are three approaches:

  • First (and strongest)—the view of the majority of the Iraqis, al-Saydalani, and others: that farness varies with difference in horizons. So places like the Hijaz, Iraq, and Khurasan are considered far because horizons differ; while nearness is when horizons do not differ, like Baghdad, Kufah, Rayy, and Qazwin—because their moonrise is the same. If one group sees it and the other does not, it is due to lack of careful observation or a temporary impediment, unlike those whose horizons differ.
  • Second: the criterion is whether the region is the same or different. If it is the same, they are “near,” otherwise “far.” This was said by al-Saymari and others.
  • Third: farness is the distance at which prayers are shortened, and nearness is less than that. This was said by al-Fawrani, Imam al-Haramayn, al-Ghazali, al-Baghawi, and others among the Khurasanis. Imam al-Haramayn even claimed agreement on it—arguing that considering horizons would require calculations and relying on astrologers, which legal principles reject, so it would be necessary to consider the prayer-shortening distance, which the Shari‘ah ties to many rulings. Al-Nawawi said this is weak, because the matter of the new moon does not relate to the prayer-shortening distance; therefore the correct approach is to consider horizons as mentioned.

Accordingly, if one doubts whether horizons agree, then those who did not see the new moon are not obligated to fast—because the default is non-obligation, and because fasting is only obligatory by sighting (as per the hadith), and sighting has not been established for these people due to lack of established nearness to the land of sighting. This is what is well-known among the companions of the Shafi‘i school in the two methodological approaches.

Al-Mawardi and al-Sarakhsi were unique in mentioning two other methodological approaches. Al-Mawardi said: if they sight it in one land and not another, there are three views:

  • First: it is binding upon those who did not see it, because the obligation of Ramadan does not differ by differing lands, and Ramadan has been established.
  • Second: it is not binding upon them, because sunrises and sunsets may differ due to differing lands, and each people are addressed with their own horizon and sunset. Do you not see that dawn may come earlier in one land and later in another? Likewise the sun may set earlier in one land and later in another. Each land considers its own dawn and sunset for its people; likewise the new moon.
  • Third: if they are within one region it is binding, otherwise not.

Al-Sarakhsi said: if the people of one area see it and another area does not, then if the distance is close, it is binding upon all of them. The criterion for “close” is that normally, if these people see it, it would not be hidden from those people except due to a temporary impediment—whether the distance is the prayer-shortening distance or something else. If the distance is far, then there are three views:

  • First: it is binding upon everyone; Abu ‘Ali al-Sinji chose this.
  • Second: it is not binding upon them.
  • Third: if the distance is such that it is not conceivable that it would be seen by these people and yet hidden from those people without an impediment, then it is binding; but if it is conceivable that it could be hidden from them, then it is not.

So the issue yields six “faces” (six outcomes):

  • First: it is binding upon every place (i.e., all lands).
  • Second: it is binding upon the people of the region of the land of sighting, but not others.
  • Third: it is binding upon every land that agrees with the land of sighting in horizon, but not others—and this is the strongest of them.
  • Fourth: it is binding upon every land for whom it is not conceivable to be hidden without an impediment, but not others (as reported by al-Sarakhsi).
  • Fifth: it is binding upon those within the prayer-shortening distance, but not others.
  • Sixth: it is not binding upon any land other than the land of sighting (as reported by al-Mawardi). And Allah, Glorified and Exalted, knows best.

Branch: Schools of Scholars on “If One Land Sights It and Others Do Not”

In our school (i.e., the Shafi‘i school), and Ibn al-Mundhir reported from ‘Ikrimah, al-Qasim, Salim, and Ishaq ibn Rahawayh that it is not binding upon anyone other than the people of the land of sighting. And from al-Layth, al-Shafi‘i, and Ahmad: it is binding upon everyone. He said: I do not know it except as the saying of the Madinan and the Kufan—meaning Malik and Abu Hanifah.(4)
